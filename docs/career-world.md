# Career, Economy & World

## 1. Career fantasy

The career should feel like a long personal climb from anonymous hobbyist to elite engineer-competitor.

The player begins with:
- little money,
- limited equipment,
- premade toy-scale hardware,
- weak measurement capability,
- local suppliers,
- local events,
- almost no reputation.

The world should not immediately treat the player as special.

Reputation is earned through results, products, engineering innovation, and repeated presence in the scene.

## 2. Career timescale

A serious first career should support **100+ hours** before the player has exhausted major progression.

The world continues after elite competition is reached.

There is no final material or final top that ends the game.

Long-term play comes from:
- evolving regulations,
- new competitors,
- technology shifts,
- business opportunities,
- rivalry,
- design iteration,
- workshop optimization,
- new side leagues,
- player-created engineering goals.

## 3. Open seasonal calendar

The career uses a seasonal calendar rather than a linear mission ladder.

Events can include:
- local club nights,
- amateur tournaments,
- regional qualifiers,
- national events,
- invitational tournaments,
- professional championship rounds,
- manufacturer cups,
- sponsor exhibitions,
- endurance events,
- experimental side leagues,
- underground high-risk events,
- engineering showcases.

The player chooses what to enter.

Skipping an event is valid.

## 4. Event requirements

Events can require:
- class eligibility,
- ranking,
- qualifying result,
- invitation,
- entry fee,
- sponsor commitment,
- technical inspection,
- safety certification.

This gives workshop decisions strategic consequences.

## 5. Main competition identity

Main career competition is elimination-based.

No judge score determines the primary result.

Loss conditions:
- spin death,
- ring-out,
- mechanical incapacitation,
- catastrophic failure.

Tournament formats can vary:
- single elimination,
- double elimination,
- round robin,
- seasonal points for standings across separate elimination matches,
- qualification brackets.

Season standings may use championship points, but **individual battles are physically decided**.

## 6. Technical classes

Different competition classes can permit different engineering envelopes.

Example class variables:
- maximum mass,
- maximum diameter,
- maximum height,
- launch-energy limit,
- permitted materials,
- permitted tip types,
- permitted active mechanisms,
- containment requirements,
- inspection requirements.

This allows:
- toy-scale beginner classes,
- tightly regulated technical classes,
- heavier professional classes,
- high-energy experimental events.

Late game can become extreme without forcing every league to become extreme.

## 7. Evolving rulebooks

Regulations can change between seasons.

Reasons:
- safety incidents,
- dominant technologies,
- new material availability,
- manufacturer lobbying,
- cost control,
- containment concerns,
- spectator safety,
- competitive balance through engineering constraints.

Changes should be announced early enough to permit engineering response.

The player can be caught with expensive obsolete hardware, but should not be randomly invalidated without warning.

## 8. Rival simulation

Competitors are persistent agents rather than level-scaled encounter templates.

Each competitor can have:
- age/career stage,
- reputation,
- budget,
- income,
- debt,
- workshop capability,
- equipment,
- suppliers,
- material access,
- design knowledge,
- preferred strategies,
- risk tolerance,
- manufacturing competence,
- launch competence,
- sponsor relationships,
- inventory,
- active projects.

They participate in the same broad technological ecosystem as the player.

## 9. Rival careers

NPCs can:
- begin as amateurs,
- improve,
- stagnate,
- change strategy,
- gain sponsors,
- lose sponsors,
- go broke,
- retire,
- return,
- join teams,
- start companies,
- become suppliers,
- become manufacturers,
- become commentators or organizers.

Important rivals can remain relevant for many seasons.

New entrants appear over time so the world does not age into emptiness.

## 10. Rival engineering

NPCs should develop designs based on:
- available technology,
- observed competition,
- their budget,
- their workshop,
- their preferences,
- their failures,
- market trends.

They should not need to run the exact same expensive CAD workflow as the player internally.

A reduced AI engineering model can generate plausible designs from the same physical rules.

The important requirement is that rival hardware obeys the same mechanical universe.

## 11. Counter-development

Rivals can adapt to the player.

Examples:
- player dominates with high-inertia stamina builds,
- rival experiments with destabilizing contact geometry,
- another rival switches to aggressive launch strategy,
- supplier demand increases for suitable materials,
- regulations may eventually respond if the technology creates safety problems.

Counters should emerge from engineering constraints rather than rubber-band stat bonuses.

## 12. Economy

Sources of income:
- tournament winnings,
- seasonal prize money,
- sponsorship,
- appearance fees,
- exhibitions,
- contracts,
- custom machining,
- part sales,
- design licensing,
- consulting-like engineering jobs,
- selling obsolete equipment.

Expenses:
- entry fees,
- raw materials,
- tooling,
- machine purchases,
- machine maintenance,
- workshop rent/upgrade,
- travel,
- destroyed hardware,
- prototype development,
- inspection,
- supplier shipping,
- sponsorship obligations,
- production inventory.

## 13. Financial danger

The career can punish reckless spending.

A player may:
- enter a machine they cannot afford to replace,
- lose a critical prototype,
- buy expensive stock and scrap it,
- break tools,
- over-invest in machinery,
- accept a bad contract,
- sit out events while rebuilding.

There should be recovery paths.

Examples:
- return to cheaper events,
- sell parts,
- do machining work,
- use older hardware,
- sell equipment,
- take a smaller sponsor,
- buy used parts.

Financial pressure should create stories, not dead-end save files.

## 14. Sponsorship

Sponsors can provide:
- money,
- material,
- machines,
- supplier access,
- travel support,
- testing resources.

In return they may require:
- event attendance,
- branding,
- product use,
- exclusivity,
- minimum results,
- appearances,
- development collaboration.

A sponsor should be a strategic relationship, not a passive +income modifier.

## 15. Marketplace

The market contains:
- commercial parts,
- used parts,
- player parts,
- rival/manufacturer parts,
- raw materials,
- machines,
- tools,
- surplus,
- scrap,
- experimental stock.

Prices can respond to:
- popularity,
- scarcity,
- supplier capacity,
- regulation,
- successful designs,
- material trends.

## 16. Player manufacturing business

The player can create products from custom designs.

A product has:
- name,
- revision,
- published specifications,
- manufacturing recipe,
- cost,
- sale price,
- quality range,
- reputation,
- adoption.

The player can choose:
- boutique high-precision production,
- cheap mass production,
- licensing,
- custom commissions,
- keeping designs private.

## 17. Technology diffusion

Successful designs influence the world.

Possible diffusion paths:
- competitors buy the player's part,
- competitors reverse-engineer ideas,
- manufacturers release similar products,
- suppliers market compatible stock,
- rulemakers respond,
- commentators discuss the trend.

Selling a design is a real strategic decision.

The player can eventually face opponents using their own technology.

## 18. Suppliers

Suppliers are persistent businesses with:
- catalogs,
- lead times,
- quality,
- trustworthiness,
- pricing,
- stock,
- specialties,
- relationships.

Supplier relationships can improve access.

A shady supplier may offer:
- cheap uncertified titanium,
- mysterious surplus,
- off-spec stock,
- experimental material.

The bargain can be excellent or disastrous.

## 19. Information ecosystem

The player learns about the world through:
- event calendars,
- forums,
- competitor profiles,
- product listings,
- supplier catalogs,
- technical bulletins,
- regulation updates,
- sponsor messages,
- rumors,
- post-event reports.

This can make the world feel alive without requiring huge cinematic story content.

## 20. Reputation

Reputation can exist in multiple dimensions:
- competitive success,
- engineering credibility,
- manufacturing quality,
- reliability,
- innovation,
- professionalism,
- risk-taking.

Different actors care about different forms of reputation.

A brilliant but destructive experimental engineer may attract a very different sponsor from a consistent mainstream champion.

## 21. Side leagues

Side leagues allow the game to loosen the main career rules.

Possibilities:
- oversized classes,
- strange arenas,
- active arena mechanisms,
- unusual materials,
- endurance formats,
- destructive events,
- prototype-only leagues,
- salvage competitions,
- team events.

This is where the game can become more theatrical without weakening the grounded main career.

## 22. Event persistence

Where rules allow, events can have persistent physical context.

Examples:
- arena surface wear across rounds,
- accumulated debris between poorly maintained underground matches,
- temperature changes,
- damage to containment,
- equipment failures.

Official events are more likely to clean, inspect, or replace surfaces between matches.

## 23. History

The game should remember:
- tournament winners,
- championship seasons,
- famous rivalries,
- major failures,
- retired machines,
- design records,
- market trends,
- regulation changes,
- player-created product success.

This history can be surfaced through:
- workshop trophies,
- archives,
- news,
- competitor bios,
- commentator references,
- product provenance.

## 24. Emergent narrative principle

The career does not need a heavily scripted protagonist story.

Its best stories should emerge from systems:

- the player finally beats a long-term rival,
- a favorite ring fails after years of fatigue,
- a sold design becomes the new meta,
- a regulation kills a dominant architecture,
- a cheap supplier batch ruins a championship attempt,
- a former amateur becomes a world-class manufacturer,
- the player returns from near bankruptcy using old hardware.

The simulation is the story generator.
